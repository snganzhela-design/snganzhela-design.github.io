2e:T207c,<p>An overview of common grafting solutions and their clinical properties. The comparison is made between <strong>material classes</strong>, not individual brands: within each class, products differ in processing, particle size and handling.</p>
<p>For a broader introduction, see <a href="/blog/dental-bone-graft-types-and-materials/">Dental Bone Graft types and materials</a>.</p>
<h2 id="topic-1">Dentin xenograft at a glance</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Ready to use:</strong> supplied sterile, no chairside preparation or second surgical site.</li>
<li><strong>No donor-site harvesting:</strong> no additional surgery to obtain graft material.</li>
<li><strong>Clinical evidence:</strong> a randomized controlled trial met its primary endpoint of non-inferiority to a porcine bone xenograft (see <a href="#topic-9">Clinical evidence</a> below).</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="topic-2">How to read this comparison</h2>
<p>Each material class is described by the same criteria:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Handling</strong> — how the graft is prepared and how easy it is to obtain;</li>
<li><strong>Safety profile</strong> — harvesting risks, immune response, sterility and the presence of cells;</li>
<li><strong>Biological properties</strong> — osteoconduction, osteoinduction and what is known about bone formation.</li>
</ul>
<p>The descriptions summarise the general literature on each class. The choice of graft for a particular patient is a clinical decision of the treating dentist or surgeon.</p>
<h2 id="topic-3">Dentin xenograft (Ivory Dentin Graft™)</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Source:</strong> dentin of porcine (animal) teeth — a xenogeneic material.</li>
<li><strong>Preparation:</strong> ready-made graft; dried, uncompacted granules that are hydrated before use.</li>
<li><strong>Availability:</strong> CE marked (MDR 2017/745), supplied through local <a href="/distributors/">distributors</a>; vials and syringes.</li>
<li><strong>Harvesting risks:</strong> none — no donor site.</li>
<li><strong>Immune response:</strong> cell-free processed dentin matrix; all biocompatibility testing concluded the material can be regarded as safe. Contraindicated in patients with known or suspected hypersensitivity to porcine products.</li>
<li><strong>Sterility:</strong> sterilized by gamma irradiation.</li>
<li><strong>Cells:</strong> no cells in the processed dentin.</li>
<li><strong>Biological properties:</strong> osteoconductive scaffold; dentin matrix with a retained organic component (type I collagen); osteoinductive potential is reported in the literature for the dentin class; slow resorption in parallel with new bone formation.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="topic-4">Dentin autograft</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Source:</strong> the patient&#39;s own extracted teeth.</li>
<li><strong>Preparation:</strong> chairside processing of the extracted teeth by the clinician with a dedicated grinding device — a technical, time-consuming step.</li>
<li><strong>Availability:</strong> depends on the teeth extracted from the same patient.</li>
<li><strong>Harvesting risks:</strong> minor — requires extracted teeth; no separate bone harvesting.</li>
<li><strong>Immune response:</strong> none expected (own tissue).</li>
<li><strong>Sterility:</strong> not applicable (own tissue, processed chairside).</li>
<li><strong>Cells:</strong> no cells in dentin.</li>
<li><strong>Biological properties:</strong> osteoconductive; osteoinductive potential reported for the dentin class; good bone formation reported in clinical studies.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="topic-5">Bone autograft</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Source:</strong> the patient&#39;s own bone, harvested surgically (intraoral or extraoral donor site).</li>
<li><strong>Preparation:</strong> harvested by the surgeon during a surgical procedure.</li>
<li><strong>Availability:</strong> limited by the volume of bone that can be harvested.</li>
<li><strong>Harvesting risks:</strong> medium — a second surgical site with possible pain, morbidity and longer surgery.</li>
<li><strong>Immune response:</strong> none expected (own tissue).</li>
<li><strong>Sterility:</strong> not applicable (own tissue).</li>
<li><strong>Cells:</strong> contains the patient&#39;s own living cells.</li>
<li><strong>Biological properties:</strong> osteogenic, osteoinductive and osteoconductive; traditionally regarded in the literature as the reference material, with faster resorption that may reduce graft volume over time.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="topic-6">Bone allograft</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Source:</strong> processed human donor (cadaver) bone from tissue banks.</li>
<li><strong>Preparation:</strong> ready-made graft.</li>
<li><strong>Availability:</strong> commercially available in a wide range of forms.</li>
<li><strong>Harvesting risks:</strong> none for the patient.</li>
<li><strong>Immune response:</strong> low; depends on processing.</li>
<li><strong>Sterility:</strong> sterilized; donor screening and processing reduce the risk of disease transmission.</li>
<li><strong>Cells:</strong> depends on processing — most processed allografts are cell-free.</li>
<li><strong>Biological properties:</strong> osteoconductive; osteoinductive potential varies with processing (for example, demineralized forms); bone formation varies by product.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="topic-7">Bone xenograft</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Source:</strong> processed animal bone (most often bovine or porcine).</li>
<li><strong>Preparation:</strong> ready-made graft.</li>
<li><strong>Availability:</strong> commercially available in a wide range of forms.</li>
<li><strong>Harvesting risks:</strong> none for the patient.</li>
<li><strong>Immune response:</strong> low; depends on processing.</li>
<li><strong>Sterility:</strong> sterilized.</li>
<li><strong>Cells:</strong> depends on processing — deproteinized products are cell-free.</li>
<li><strong>Biological properties:</strong> osteoconductive scaffold with slow resorption; osteoinductive properties are generally not attributed to deproteinized bone.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="topic-8">Synthetic graft</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Source:</strong> synthetic materials — calcium phosphates (hydroxyapatite, β-tricalcium phosphate), bioactive glass and their combinations.</li>
<li><strong>Preparation:</strong> ready-made graft.</li>
<li><strong>Availability:</strong> commercially available.</li>
<li><strong>Harvesting risks:</strong> none.</li>
<li><strong>Immune response:</strong> none expected for biocompatible materials.</li>
<li><strong>Sterility:</strong> sterilized.</li>
<li><strong>Cells:</strong> no cells.</li>
<li><strong>Biological properties:</strong> osteoconductive; further biological activity depends on composition and additives; bone formation and resorption vary by material.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="topic-9">Clinical evidence for Ivory Dentin Graft</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Primary endpoint:</strong> in a randomized controlled clinical trial, Ivory Dentin Graft was non-inferior to a porcine-derived bone xenograft (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10264522/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">PMC10264522</a>).</li>
<li><strong>Secondary outcomes (histology, same trial):</strong> good graft integration in 85% of sites vs 40% (p = 0.0066); new bone at re-entry 60.75% vs 42.81% (p = 0.0084) — numerically higher than the comparator.</li>
<li><strong>Dentin class:</strong> a meta-analysis of dentin-graft studies reported +12.4% new bone compared with bone xenografts (p &lt; 0.001, high certainty of evidence) (<a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12149069/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">PMC12149069</a>). This result refers to dentin grafts as a class, not to a specific product.</li>
<li><strong>Limitations we state ourselves:</strong> the trial was sponsored by the manufacturer, conducted at a single centre and included 36 patients.</li>
</ul>
<p>Publications and clinical cases are collected in <a href="/education/">Education</a>.</p>
<h2 id="topic-10">Choosing a graft</h2>
<p>Each material class has its place in bone reconstruction.
